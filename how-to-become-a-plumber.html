How to Become a Plumber (2026): Apprenticeship to Master

Plumbing's path is the most standardized in the trades — apprentice, journeyman, master, with hours and exams at each gate. That structure makes it very plannable: here's the sequence, the decisions that actually matter, and where people lose time.

Step 1: Get into a registered apprenticeship

The apprenticeship is both your training and your income — treat securing it as the whole game in year one. Your options:

  • Union apprenticeship (UA — the United Association's local programs): competitive entry (aptitude test, interview), ~five years structured, near-zero tuition, wages stepping up annually, classroom bundled.
  • Merit-shop / non-union apprenticeship (ABC and independent programs registered with the state or US Department of Labor): same legal structure, different culture and entry speed.
  • Working helper + state registration: in some states you can register as a plumbing apprentice directly with the board and learn under a licensed plumber — slower on theory, immediate on income.

Apply to more than one program; slots are the constraint, not your qualifications.

Step 2: Log the hours — the license is built on them

Journeyman requires documented supervised hours (commonly 4,000–8,500 depending on state) plus classroom hours. The discipline that separates on-time plumbers from late ones: verify your hours get recorded with the board monthly, and make sure you're getting breadth (drain work, water piping, fixtures, gas if your state includes it) — missing categories delay applications even when total hours are fine.

Step 3: Pass the journeyman exam

The exam tests your state's adopted plumbing code — UPC or IPC, know which — plus theory and math (isometrics, drain sizing, venting). Administered via PSI, ICC, or the board. Prep: the code book, a tabbed-and-annotated copy, and timed practice lookups. Fees roughly $50–$350.

Step 4: The master/contractor fork

As a journeyman you can work under a licensed contractor's umbrella. The fork:

  • Master tier: more documented years (commonly one to two+) plus the master exam — required in many states to supervise apprentices or run projects independently.
  • Contractor license: the business tier — exams (trade + business-and-law), insurance, bonding, permits. See the state requirements map for your state's structure.

Step 5: Specialization and income growth

Service plumbing (diagnosis-and-repair) pays differently from new-construction rough-in; medical gas, backflow prevention certification, and commercial/industrial work are the classic premium niches — each adds a certification layer on top of the license, not a new license.

Timeline and cost, honestly

  • Journeyman: ~4–5 years standard; faster where state hour minimums sit at the low end and your documentation is aggressive.
  • Master/contractor: +1–3 years beyond journeyman.
  • Cash cost: near-zero through union apprenticeship to $10k+ through private schools; exam/licensing fees are the minor line.

General information, not career or legal advice. Verify hour counts, code editions, and exam requirements with your state plumbing board. Wage data: BLS Plumbers, Pipefitters, and Steamfitters.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to become a plumber?

About four to five years from zero to journeyman in most states — driven by supervised-hour requirements (commonly 4,000–8,500 hours) plus classroom instruction, then the journeyman exam. Master and contractor tiers add one to two or more years of journeyman experience plus further exams.

Do I need trade school to become a plumber?

No. The dominant route is a registered apprenticeship (union UA programs or merit-shop programs), which pays you while you train and covers classroom hours. Trade school can accelerate theory and help you get hired, but most states' licenses are built on documented supervised hours plus exams, not diplomas.

Is plumbing a good career compared to other trades?

It trades slightly longer training than HVAC for the most consistent statewide licensing structure of the major trades — which historically supports stronger wage floors for licensed journeymen and steadier demand (sanitation is non-discretionary). The BLS plumbing page has current wage and outlook data by state.

What's the difference between journeyman and master plumber?

Journeyman is the working license: you can perform plumbing independently under the trade rules of your state. Master is the advanced tier — more documented years plus a harder exam — and in most states it (or a contractor license built on it) is what lets you run a plumbing business, pull permits, and supervise apprentices.
