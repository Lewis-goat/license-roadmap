Electrician licensing looks chaotic from the outside — fifty states, different hour counts, exams, and reciprocity — but it resolves into a clear pattern once you see it: most states license electricians statewide in tiers; a well-defined group leaves it to cities; and almost everyone licenses the contractor. This guide maps all three groups, the hour expectations, and the boards to verify with.

Group 1: Statewide tiered licensing (the majority)

The classic structure: apprentice → journeyman → master (sometimes with a residential-only track). Expectations in these states:

  • Apprentice: registered with the state or with a US Department of Labor–approved program (IBEW/NECA joint apprenticeships, ABC, or independent schools). Typically 4–5 years, combining paid on-the-job hours with classroom instruction.
  • Journeyman: requires documented on-the-job hours (commonly in the 4,000–8,000 hour range — two to four years full-time) plus a pass on the state journeyman exam, which tests the National Electrical Code (NEC) and theory.
  • Master / contractor: additional years as a journeyman (commonly 1–2+ years), a business-and-law exam alongside the technical exam, and the insurance/bonding that contractor registration requires.

Strict-employee states to know: California requires electricians working for a C-10 licensed contractor to hold a state certification (via DIR) — hours plus exam — not just the contractor. Texas requires worker registration even where local licensing applies.

Boards worth bookmarking: Texas TDLR, Florida DBPR, California DIR electrician certification, Washington L&I.

Group 2: Local licensing states

In a set of states — frequently listed as New York, Pennsylvania, Illinois (except Chicago), Kansas, Missouri, and Wyoming — there is no statewide journeyman electrician license. What that means in practice:

  • You comply with each city or county where you work: some (NYC, Philadelphia, Chicago) run demanding master-electrician exams; smaller jurisdictions may require only a simple registration or nothing beyond permits.
  • Contractor licensing can still be statewide in some of these states, so the business layer and the worker layer follow different rules.
  • Moving between cities in these states can mean re-testing. Plan around it if you intend to relocate.

Group 3: The universal layer (every state)

  • NEC adoption: every state bases its electrical code on the National Electrical Code, though states adopt different edition years. The exam you take almost everywhere is NEC-centric.
  • Permits and inspections: licensing governs who may work; permits govern what work gets inspected. Licensed or not, permitted work needs a permit-holder.
  • Continuing education: many states tie renewal to CEU hours (commonly 4–8 hours per cycle) — relevant once you hold the license.

How to verify your state (5 minutes)

  1. Search your state + electrician license and prefer the .gov result (state licensing department or electrical board).
  2. Confirm: does the state license journeyman electricians, contractors only, or neither?
  3. Get the exact hour requirement for the tier you're targeting and the exam administrator (PSI, Prometric, ICC, or state-run).
  4. Check reciprocity if you might move — several state boards honor each other's journeyman licenses.

This guide is general information, not legal or career advice. Hour counts, exam structures, and even the statewide/local split change over time — verify with your state's board before registering for anything.

Frequently asked questions

Which states have NO statewide electrician license?

A group of states — commonly cited examples include New York, Pennsylvania, Illinois (outside Chicago), Kansas, Missouri, and Wyoming — leave electrician licensing to cities and counties. New York City has one of the strictest master electrician systems in the country even though New York State has no statewide license.

Does California require electricians to be certified even as employees?

Yes. California is the strict example: electricians working for a C-10 contractor must be certified with the state (DIR) once they exceed apprentice status, with hour requirements and an exam — unlike most contractor-license states where only the contracting business is licensed.

How many hours does a journeyman electrician license require?

It varies widely: roughly 4,000 hours (about two years) on the low end to 8,000 hours (about four years) plus classroom hours on the high end. Several states are around the 8,000-hour mark for journeyman. Verify your state's exact hours with its licensing board — guides go stale, boards don't.

Do I need a license to do electrical work on my own house?

Most states exempt homeowners doing work on their own primary residence from licensing, though permits are still required for significant work. This exemption never applies to property you rent out or sell — that's contracting.
